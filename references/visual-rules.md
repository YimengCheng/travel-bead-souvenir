# Visual Rules Reference

## Canvas and page structure

- Use a 3:4 portrait canvas.
- Keep the user's real travel photo in the upper section and the bead artwork in the lower section.
- Use a warm cream or matte ivory background.
- The lower bead work does not need to fill its section or form a complete rectangle.

## What “good” should feel like

The goal is a refined travel-souvenir object photographed on warm cream paper. The best outputs feel edited, selective, and tactile. They do not look like a whole photo passed through a pixel-art filter.

## Negative space

Use more empty cream background than feels necessary at first. A common failure mode is making the lower bead piece too large. Start around 55–60% of the lower width and enlarge only when the composition truly needs it.

The bead subject will usually occupy about 45–70% of the lower area. Vary scale, placement, and breathing room from page to page so the series has rhythm.

## Bead size control

- Use a bead scale that preserves the defining silhouette without reproducing every tiny detail.
- Enlarge individual beads when the subject needs a bolder, more graphic interpretation.
- Use smaller beads only when a landmark façade or spatial layer would otherwise become unreadable.
- Keep bead diameter and spacing physically coherent within one artwork.

## Useful 3D strategies

Use 3D only when there is a real reason:

- **Courtyard**: floor plane extends forward, fountain lifts independently, arcade sits behind.
- **Waterfront**: water base lies horizontal, boats pop up, architecture steps backward.
- **Village landscape**: fields form front layers, vegetation creates midground, village rises as the back layer.
- **Road sign**: signpost and lamps can stand as a compact object; do not rebuild an entire street.
- **Animal pair**: animals can be independent bead silhouettes with a small patch of leaves/water, not a full rectangular pond.

Avoid a repeated “upright backdrop + slanted floor” diorama for every scene.

Do not make a person or isolated object protrude unless that protrusion follows the real scene's spatial structure.

## When flat is better

Keep flat when the scene depends on graphic light rather than physical depth:

- skyline sunset;
- silhouettes;
- night fair lights;
- a broad horizon;
- bold reflections;
- a dense stained-glass interior if depth would make it too busy.

## Cropping the upper real photo

For portrait originals, a horizontal crop is allowed when it improves the top panel. Preserve the decisive subject and do not crop out the feature that makes the scene recognizable. Never stretch or synthesize missing edges.

## Original-photo fidelity

The upper section must remain the actual user-uploaded photograph. Allow only light exposure, white-balance, contrast, saturation, and tonal adjustments, plus reasonable cropping. Do not AI-repaint it; change people, buildings, animals, or weather; invent missing objects; or wash the photograph into an obvious AI image.

## Color extraction

- Identify the original scene's dominant 4–10 colors.
- Preserve the main warm/cool relationships and the colors that make the subject recognizable.
- Remove muddy intermediate colors and low-value background noise.
- Do not oversaturate colors that were muted in the original photograph.
- Keep the warm-cream paper and bead material consistent across a series while allowing each destination's palette to differ.

## Decorative restraint

Do not add polaroids, stamps, postcards, leaves, tape, plants, craft scraps, or slogans just because a reference image contains them. Borrow the reference image's spacing, scale, and physical depth—not its props.

Also avoid mechanical flowers, stickers, handwriting, or any random travel decoration. Do not copy the visual elements of a reference creator.

## Page-to-page rhythm

Keep 3:4 format, cream background, photographic fidelity, fuse-bead material, and overall design language consistent. Vary bead size, flat/irregular/semi-3D/3D structure, subject position, frame-breaking, and whether the piece forms a complete rectangle. Each page should feel like a distinct souvenir within the same travel album, not one template with a different photo.

## Signs of over-processing

Reject/revise if the upper photo has:

- changed facial features;
- altered animal anatomy;
- rebuilt architecture;
- fake extra clouds or trees;
- overly smooth textures;
- exaggerated HDR;
- AI-looking edge details;
- unreadable or fabricated signs.
